Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Helium gas or liquid nitrogen for the first thermal shield of a 4 K cryostat: conjugate heat-transfer budget, coolant operating maps and work ledger", for publication in Thermal Science and Engineering Progress as a full-length article.

The first thermal shield of a 4 K cryostat can be cooled by circulated helium gas, as in the ITER and LHC cryostats, or by liquid nitrogen, as in magnet cryostats. The choice sets the heat load that reaches the 4 K stage and the room-temperature power needed to hold it. The manuscript separates this choice into three questions and answers each with a dedicated model: (1) the passive heat-load penalty of a 77 K shield relative to a 50 K shield, from a three-dimensional multi-region conjugate heat-transfer model that resolves view-factor radiation and the conduction of a 65-line coaxial harness; (2) the performance of the two coolants in a channelled cold plate over Reynolds numbers 500 to 10,000 and helium pressures of 1 to 18 bar, on three bases of comparison, with grid-convergence studies for both fluids and NIST-referenced properties; and (3) a room-temperature work ledger with ideal Carnot multipliers and with published hardware efficiencies, including the effect of multi-layer insulation and emissivity uncertainty.

The main findings are that the penalty of the warmer shield appears entirely at the 4 K stage and is predominantly conductive, so it is a property of the wiring rather than of the cryogen; that the two coolants rank differently at matched Reynolds number, matched mass flow and matched pumping power, so single-basis comparisons are misleading; that helium at atmospheric pressure lets the cold plate float well above its nominal temperature at low flow, while pressurised helium recovers a pumping-power advantage; and that the ideal and the hardware-based ledgers give opposite verdicts, which the paper reconciles through an explicit break-even condition on stage efficiencies.

An earlier version of this work was reviewed at Cryogenics. The present manuscript is a substantial revision: the scope has been narrowed and reframed to the cryostat first shield, the helium properties have been corrected to NIST values, a fourteen-run parametric campaign and grid studies for both fluids have been added, and the interpretation has been rewritten to distinguish component performance, ideal thermodynamics and practical hardware. All OpenFOAM cases, scripts and tabulated results are openly available in the linked repository.
